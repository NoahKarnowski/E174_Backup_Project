1.1 Load Raw CSV Files

Code 
# OECD data: US GDP, Domestic Demand, and Imports of Goods & Services
# Period: 2000 Q1 to 2025 Q4
# Units: national currency, chain-linked volume, base year = 2017
# Unit multiplier = 6 (true values = observation × 10^6)

US_DD  <- read.csv("us_dd.csv")
US_IMP <- read.csv("us_imp.csv")
US_GDP <- read.csv("us_gdp.csv")

1.2 Clean and Join into Tsibble

Code 
US_DD <- US_DD %>%
  dplyr::select(date = TIME_PERIOD, country = REF_AREA,
                domestic_demand = OBS_VALUE)

US_GDP <- US_GDP %>%
  dplyr::select(date = TIME_PERIOD, country = REF_AREA,
                gdp = OBS_VALUE)

US_IMP <- US_IMP %>%
  dplyr::select(date = TIME_PERIOD, country = REF_AREA,
                imports = OBS_VALUE)

us_clean <- US_DD %>%
  left_join(US_GDP, by = c("date", "country")) %>%
  left_join(US_IMP, by = c("date", "country")) %>%
  mutate(date = yearquarter(date)) %>%
  group_by(date, country) %>%
  summarise(
    domestic_demand = sum(domestic_demand, na.rm = TRUE),
    gdp             = sum(gdp,             na.rm = TRUE),
    imports         = sum(imports,         na.rm = TRUE),
    .groups = "drop"
  ) %>%
  as_tsibble(key = country, index = date)

# Add log-transformed columns — stabilises variance and linearises growth
us_clean <- us_clean %>%
  mutate(
    log_gdp             = log(gdp),
    log_domestic_demand = log(domestic_demand),
    log_imports         = log(imports)
  )

2 Exploratory Analysis

2.1 Raw Level Plots

Analysis: All three series exhibit clear upward trends with a visible contraction around 2008–09 (Global Financial Crisis) and a sharp drop in 2020 Q2 (COVID-19 pandemic). Imports show the most volatility of the three. The upward drift and variance behaviour motivate a log transformation before modelling.

2.2 Log-Transformed Series

Analysis: Log transformation produces more linear growth paths. Variance instability is reduced but the COVID shock remains a prominent outlier in all three series — this will affect residual diagnostics across all models.

2.3 STL Decomposition

Analysis: All three series share the same decomposition structure: a dominant upward trend, a small but consistent quarterly seasonal pattern (amplitude ≈ ±0.002 for GDP and domestic demand, ≈ ±0.005 for imports), and a remainder capturing the 2008–09 recession and the 2020 COVID collapse. The seasonal component is stable in amplitude (no growth), suggesting additive seasonality on the log scale. The relatively small seasonal amplitude compared to trend means seasonal differencing may not always improve fit — model selection via AICc will determine whether D=1 is warranted.

4 ETS Modelling

ETS (Error–Trend–Seasonal) models are state-space models that decompose a series into three exponentially smoothed components. Because all series are log-transformed, additive ETS on the log scale is equivalent to multiplicative ETS on the original scale.

4.1 Fit Multiple ETS Specifications

Five specifications are fit per variable: an automatically selected model, two damped/undamped additive variants, and one full additive seasonal model. Residuals are checked before using the models for forecasting to confirm white-noise assumptions.

Code

4.3 ETS Residual Diagnostics

Residuals are checked on the auto-selected model (best AICc candidate) before forecasting. A well-specified model should show: (1) no autocorrelation in the time plot, (2) ACF spikes within bounds, and (3) a roughly normal histogram.

Analysis: Examine each panel. The 2020 Q2 COVID shock will appear as a large spike in the time plot and may widen the histogram tails — this is a structural break that ETS cannot capture. If ACF lags remain within bounds (aside from the outlier period), the white-noise assumption is broadly satisfied. Any significant ACF structure at seasonal lags (lag 4, 8) would suggest a seasonal ETS component is needed.

5 ARIMA Modelling

ARIMA models use lagged values and lagged errors to capture autocorrelation structure after differencing to achieve stationarity. The differencing order d is determined by unit root tests; seasonal differencing D is evaluated via AICc.

5.1 Unit Root Tests and Differencing Diagnostics

5.1.1 ACF of Log Levels

Analysis: A slow, linear decay in the ACF is the hallmark of a non-stationary (unit root) process. All three series should show this pattern — confirming that d ≥ 1 is needed before fitting ARIMA.

5.1.3 ACF and PACF of First-Differenced Series

After applying d=1, the ACF and PACF of the differenced series reveal the AR and MA orders.

Analysis: After first-differencing, the series should be approximately stationary. Read the ACF and PACF to inform manual model specs: - ACF cuts off at lag q, PACF decays → MA(q) process - PACF cuts off at lag p, ACF decays → AR(p) process - Both decay → ARMA(p,q) - Spikes at seasonal lags (4, 8, 12) → seasonal terms needed

Check whether ACF at lag 4 is significant — if so, a seasonal MA term PDQ(0,1,1) is warranted.

6 VAR Modelling

VAR (Vector Autoregression) jointly models all three variables as a system, allowing each variable’s forecast to be informed by the lagged values of the others. This captures cross-variable dynamics (e.g., imports responding to GDP growth with a lag) that univariate ETS and ARIMA cannot.

VAR requires stationary inputs. Because all three log series have unit roots (d=1), we work with first-differenced log series. An alternative cointegration framework (VECM) is beyond the scope of this project.

6.1 Prepare Differenced Data

Code 
us_clean_diff <- us_clean %>%
  mutate(
    d_log_gdp     = difference(log_gdp),
    d_log_dd      = difference(log_domestic_demand),
    d_log_imports = difference(log_imports)
  ) %>%
  filter(!is.na(d_log_gdp))

us_train_diff <- us_clean_diff %>% filter(date <= yearquarter("2023 Q4"))
us_test_diff  <- us_clean_diff %>% filter(date >= yearquarter("2024 Q1"))

# Remove tsibble key for vars package compatibility
train_mat <- us_train_diff %>%
  as_tibble() %>%

8 Conclusion

Summary of Findings:

All three US national accounts series — GDP, domestic demand, and imports — exhibit strong upward trends with stable quarterly seasonality and remainder variance dominated by two structural breaks: the 2008–09 Global Financial Crisis and the 2020 Q2 COVID-19 collapse. The log transformation effectively stabilises variance, and first-differencing achieves stationarity as confirmed by KPSS tests.

ETS models capture the trend-seasonal structure well. The auto-selected specification will likely be an AAN or AAA model. The primary limitation is that ETS treats the COVID outlier as a one-period shock and reverts to trend quickly — this can produce overconfident forecasts if the post-COVID recovery trajectory differs from pre-pandemic trend.

ARIMA models additionally capture short-run autocorrelation structure in the differenced series. Seasonal ARIMA specifications may improve on purely non-seasonal models given the confirmed quarterly seasonal pattern in STL. ARIMA tends to produce better short-horizon forecasts for series with residual autocorrelation after differencing.

VAR captures cross-variable dynamics. GDP, domestic demand, and imports are theoretically linked — import demand depends on domestic demand and income (GDP). The VAR’s advantage is that lagged values of one variable can improve forecasts of another. On the differenced scale, this may matter most for imports, which is the most volatile of the three series.

The COVID structural break is the primary caveat for all three models. Intervention modelling or out-of-sample evaluation restricted to the post-2021 recovery period would provide a cleaner picture of forecasting performance in the current macroeconomic environment.
